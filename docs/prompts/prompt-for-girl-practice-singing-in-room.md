    Question: Are "composition", "framing", and "perspective" the same thing? If not, how are they different?
    No, "composition," "framing," and "perspective" are related concepts in visual art and photography, but they are not the same thing.
    - **Composition** refers to the overall arrangement of visual elements within the frame, including the placement of subjects, objects, and background elements to create a balanced and aesthetically pleasing image. Example: In a photograph of a landscape, the composition might include the rule of thirds, leading lines, and the placement of the horizon to create a visually appealing scene.
    - **Framing** refers to how the subject is positioned within the boundaries of the image, including the use of foreground and background elements to create a sense of depth and context. Example: In a portrait photograph, the subject might be framed by a doorway or window, which adds context and visual interest to the image.
    - **Perspective** refers to the viewpoint or angle from which the scene is observed, which can affect the perceived depth, scale, and spatial relationships between objects in the image. Example: A low-angle perspective looking up at a tall building can make it appear more imposing, while a high-angle perspective looking down can make the subject appear smaller and more vulnerable.

    Question: Are "Camera angle" and "perspective" the same thing? If not, how are they different?
    No, "Camera angle" and "perspective" are related but distinct concepts in photography and visual storytelling.
    - **Camera Angle** refers to the specific position and orientation of the camera relative to the subject    and scene. It describes how the camera is tilted, rotated, or positioned to capture the subject from a particular viewpoint. Example: A high-angle shot looks down on the subject, while a low-angle shot looks up at the subject.
    - **Perspective** refers to the perceived spatial relationships and depth in the image, which can be influenced by the camera angle but also by other factors such as lens choice, distance from the subject, and the arrangement of elements within the frame. Perspective affects how objects appear in relation to each other, including their size, shape, and position. Example: A wide-angle lens can exaggerate perspective, making objects closer to the camera appear larger and more prominent, while a telephoto lens can compress perspective, making distant objects appear closer together.

    So "Composition", "Framing", "Camera Angle", and "Perspective" are all related but distinct concepts in visual art and photography. They each contribute to how an image is constructed and perceived, but they focus on different aspects of the visual experience.

    Example of "composition" in a photograph: A photograph of a person standing on a beach at sunset, with the sun positioned in the upper left corner of the frame, the person placed off-center to the right, and the waves leading the viewer's eye towards the horizon. The composition creates a sense of balance and visual interest.

    Example of "framing" in a photograph: A photograph of a person standing in a doorway, with the doorframe creating a natural border around the subject. The framing draws attention to the person and adds context to the scene.

    Example of "perspective" in a photograph: A photograph taken from a low angle looking up at a tall building, making it appear more imposing. The perspective affects the perceived depth and scale of the building in relation to the viewer.

    Example of "camera angle" in a photograph: A photograph taken from a high angle looking down at a person sitting on a bench, making them appear smaller and more vulnerable. The camera angle influences how the subject is perceived in relation to the viewer.
